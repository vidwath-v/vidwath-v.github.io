Experience

Software Engineering Intern, Ziplyne

July – Sept. 2026, about 20 hours a week

Ziplyne builds AI agents that sit on top of enterprise software like Workday and handle HR tasks for companies, including Johns Hopkins. I was one of five software engineering interns (about one in five applicants got in), and our job was to figure out where the agents were breaking.

  • Wrote and ran somewhere between 200 and 300 test cases on the company's demo site, which kicked off tests in Workday and tracked how the agents moved through each task.
  • The bugs weren't always obvious. We found problems with data-driven tests (DDTs), visual and manual assertions, step timing, pages that didn't finish loading, steps that got shuffled out of order, and test documentation that didn't match what the product actually did.
  • Some failures only showed up some of the time. So I built a timetable comparing execution speeds across several test frameworks, which let us say how often a problem happened instead of just calling it "flaky."
  • Helped lead our presentation to the full software engineering team, about 15 people. Telling engineers what's wrong with code they wrote is a lot of pressure as a high schooler. They used our findings to build a new version for clients.
  • Sent our data on the test-case process straight to the product manager.
  • Made 100 to 200 video test cases for their new digital adoption testing software, videos that walk a user through what to do next.
  • Worked in Java and Python and committed code to the company's GitHub repository.

Projects

Fabergé Egg Stroboscope

2025, independent project

An egg that tells the story of Rasputin. Inside, a disc of drawings spins on a DC motor while a strobe light flashes once per frame, so the drawings look like they're moving. Fig. 1 at the top of this page is a simplified version.

  • Seven interchangeable discs, one for each act of the story, with six frames on each.
  • An Arduino controls both the motor speed and the strobe timing. I wrote the code.
  • The flash rate has to match the disc exactly: rotations per second times frames per disc. If it's even slightly off, the picture drifts. I used potentiometers to dial in the output by hand.
  • The circuit runs on a breadboard with diodes, NMOS transistors, and capacitors. I simulated it in Tinkercad before building anything real.
  • It took over 50 hours, and a lot of that was failing. The code broke first. Then I couldn't get the power right. Then came the hardest part: the egg is curved, so fitting the strobe at the top and the motors, potentiometer, and circuit board at the bottom took a lot of measuring and remeasuring.
  • I doubted I'd finish it more than once. I finished it.
